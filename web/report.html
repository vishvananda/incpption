Native compilation study · 16 November 2026

Where compilation
costs go

A profile of cppgm and Clang compiling 16 LLVM source units at -O2. The sample points to semantic analysis and optimization as major contributors to the instruction gap.

Separate from the browser demo. This study used native cppgm 0.9.3 and Clang 22.1.2 on a Xeon E5-2696 v4. The demo uses cppgm 0.9.4 and runs WebAssembly on your device. These results are not browser timings or a universal speedup.

01 / Whole compile

The measured totals

Across the sample, callgrind counted 214.7 billion instructions for Clang and 59.8 billion for cppgm: about 3.6× as many for Clang. A separate perf run measured 288.4 billion cycles for Clang and 52.3 billion for cppgm, about 5.5× as many. Cycle counts came from a host under load.

03 / What the profiles suggest

The costs are uneven

Semantic analysis

Profile attribution points to type representation, expression bookkeeping, lookup, overload resolution and templates as substantial costs. Some of this work supports diagnostics and language features.

Optimization

LLVM ran more passes and analyses in this sample. Its optimizer was estimated at 49.8 G instructions versus 12.2 G for cppgm. Similar IR sizes do not establish equivalent optimization or output quality.

Lexing and parsing

Profiling found more instructions but fewer sampled cycles in cppgm. The phase boundaries differ: cppgm’s parser classifies names and builds a tree; Clang charges much of that work to semantic analysis.

04 / Evidence & limits

How to read the result

The inputs were 16 sampled LLVM 22.1.2 units, built with CI flags and libstdc++ 15 headers. The four largest units accounted for 66% of Clang’s instructions and 60% of cppgm’s, so this sample is concentrated. Clang was built at -O3, statically linked, with symbols.

What was measured and estimated?

This report summarizes local profiling; the full raw profiles are not published here. Callgrind provided whole-compile instruction counts and per-function profiles. Perf supplied phase counts and cycles. Clang’s phases were measured in separate modes; cppgm’s were estimated from its timing shares, with optimizer cost inferred from its -O2 back half less the -O0 lowering and object-writing cost. Source-file categories and charging shared code to callers are approximations; inlined call counts can be missing.

How comparable was the work?

The counts were similar, not identical: on 12 units Clang recorded 70,419 class instantiations and cppgm 65,107. Before optimization the IR counts were 472,926 LLVM instructions and 477,253 LowIR instructions; afterwards they were 195,009 and 210,469. These are different representations and counting methods. Similar volumes do not prove equal semantics, diagnostics or generated code quality.

What could Clang and LLVM improve?

The study proposes changes below. The figures are differences between attributed costs for the 16 -O2 units, as a share of Clang’s 214.7 G callgrind instructions. They illustrate opportunities if costs reached cppgm’s level; they are not savings measured from implemented changes or predictions of time saved.

Selected comparison scenarios · G = billion instructions
Proposed changeCost differenceClang total
Share type information and reduce spelling records22.4 G10.4%
Direct context navigation and cached context facts15.9 G7.4%
Reject overload candidates earlier11.4 G5.3%
Reuse canonical template substitutions9.6 G4.5%
Reuse checked facts for instantiated members6.7 G3.1%
Simpler IR attributes, value handles and metadata20.2 G9.4%
Skip unchanged passes and retain valid analyses19.5 G9.1%

Other suggestions include reducing expression bookkeeping, caching diagnostic state, running warning analyses only when needed, and selecting machine instructions more directly. Keeping type spelling, rejected candidates and warning analysis supports useful diagnostics. Shallower optimization and a simpler back end may affect generated code; this study did not compare code quality.

These cost differences do not form a set of tested patches. Do not sum them into a predicted speedup: proposed changes can overlap, and cppgm’s cost does not prove that Clang can match it while keeping its capabilities. Profile-guided optimization and BOLT might improve code layout, but their recovery was not measured.

What could GCC investigate?

In an earlier 60-unit -O0 campaign, GCC’s -ftime-report timers attributed about 16% of its compile to overload resolution, 27% to template instantiation and body checking, 24% to the back end, and 5% to garbage collection. These are timer shares, not measured savings or estimates from the 16-unit -O2 comparison.

The study suggests earlier overload filtering, less repeated template and type work, a simpler -O0 back end with direct object writing, and phase-lifetime arenas. Each change needs validation for correctness, diagnostics and output quality.

What could cppgm improve?

The study suggests simplifying token conversion and passing parser name bindings to semantic analysis to avoid duplicate lookup. It also considers reducing lexing instructions. These are small opportunities in this sample: lexing and parsing already execute at high instructions per cycle.

What about an LLVM combination?

A hypothetical cppgm front end combined with LLVM costs on Clang’s own IR was estimated at 131.7–148.2 G cycles at -O2. This was not an implemented or measured compiler combination. The range depends on assumptions about IR generation; cppgm-produced LLVM IR would also need appropriate metadata for comparable output.

What this study cannot establish

It does not establish runtime performance or code quality. Clang supports broader diagnostics, tooling and targets; those capabilities can have costs. The measured ratios depend on source, flags, versions and hardware, and loaded-host cycle counts are especially sensitive to conditions.
